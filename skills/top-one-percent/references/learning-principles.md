# Evidence-Informed Learning Principles

Use this reference when designing the curriculum, lesson sequence, review plan,
practice tasks, or feedback loop. These principles improve the default teaching
method; they do not replace domain expertise, accessibility accommodations,
professional coaching, or safety requirements.

## 1. Retrieve After Learning, Then Correct

**Principle:** Ask learners to recall, solve, explain, or perform without
looking before giving the answer. Follow with correct feedback and a short
restudy step when needed.

**Apply:** End each unit with a no-notes retrieval prompt. Revisit important
material after a delay and in a changed context. Record success, error type,
and confidence—not just whether the learner saw the material.

**Do not:** Mistake recognition (“that looks familiar”) for recall, turn every
retrieval task into a high-stakes test, or leave plausible errors uncorrected.

**Evidence:** Retrieval practice enhanced later retention in controlled studies
and also supported conceptual science learning. [Karpicke & Roediger
(2008)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408), [Karpicke & Blunt
(2011)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21252317/), and [Rowland's meta-analysis
(2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/).

## 2. Space Important Retrieval and Practice

**Principle:** Spread meaningful encounters with important material across time
instead of doing all review in one sitting. The right interval depends on the
retention goal, prior performance, and time available; no universal flashcard
schedule is justified.

**Apply:** Maintain a review queue. Revisit a skill after a meaningful delay,
then lengthen the interval following strong independent performance and shorten
it after errors. Combine a revisit with retrieval or use, not mere rereading.

**Do not:** Claim that cramming is never useful for a short deadline, or impose
fixed intervals when a learner has a different performance date.

**Evidence:** A broad review judged distributed practice and practice testing
high-utility across many conditions, while emphasizing that benefits depend on
materials, learners, and outcome tasks. [Dunlosky et al.
(2013)](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/pspi/1529100612453266/).

## 3. Show a Model, Then Fade Support

**Principle:** For a novice facing a complex procedure, use a worked example
that exposes decisions and common traps. Follow it with a completion task, a
near-transfer task, and then independent performance as competence grows.

**Apply:** Mark every consequential decision in a model. Ask the learner to
explain why each step is warranted, then remove one support at a time. Switch
away from full worked examples when they add no value or become redundant.

**Do not:** Substitute passive example watching for practice, or retain
beginner scaffolding after it slows an experienced learner.

**Evidence:** Worked examples can improve subsequent performance for novices,
but instructional load and prior knowledge matter. [Sweller
(1991)](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0804_2), [Retnowati et al.
(2009)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2008.03.006).

## 4. Require Self-Explanation and Error Diagnosis

**Principle:** Make learners explain the reasoning, causal mechanism, or choice
behind an answer, then identify the source of an error and how they will change
the next attempt.

**Apply:** Ask “why this method?”, “what assumption made it valid?”, “where did
the approach first diverge?”, and “what would change the answer?” Accept brief
explanations only when they reveal the relevant reasoning.

**Do not:** Demand elaborate narration that overwhelms a novice, or grade
eloquence instead of understanding.

**Evidence:** Prompted self-explanation improved understanding in a controlled
study of expository science text. [Chi et al.
(1994)](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3).

## 5. Make Practice Resemble the Desired Performance, Then Vary It

**Principle:** Assess and practice the thinking and performance the learner
ultimately needs. Once foundations are stable, mix related problem types and
contexts so the learner must choose a method rather than copy the last one.

**Apply:** Define the target task before designing drills. Start with blocked,
well-labeled practice for a new skill; introduce interleaving only when the
learner can identify the categories and justify method selection. Include near
transfer before far transfer.

**Do not:** Equate a high score on a familiar drill with transfer, or interleave
unrelated novice material merely to make work harder.

**Evidence:** The value of practice depends on its relationship to the later
performance test; interleaving is promising but was judged less broadly
established than retrieval and spacing in the major review. [Transfer-appropriate
processing framework](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2014/2014_Karpicke_Lehman_Aue_PLM.pdf), [Dunlosky et al.
(2013)](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/pspi/1529100612453266/).

## 6. Use Criterion-Based Feedback and Calibration

**Principle:** Feedback should compare work to a clear target, identify the
relevant task or process gap, and direct the next attempt. Learners should
predict their performance before feedback, then compare the prediction with the
result.

**Apply:** Before an assessment, ask for a probability estimate and brief
rationale. Afterward, report the evidence, the first important error, the
process change, and the next drill. Track both performance and calibration over
time.

**Do not:** Use praise, a score, or generic encouragement as the whole feedback
loop. Do not treat a learner's confidence as evidence of competence.

**Evidence:** Feedback effects vary by type and delivery; the influential review
distinguishes task, process, and self-regulation feedback. Studies of judgments
of learning show that monitoring can be biased. [Hattie & Timperley
(2007)](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487), [Koriat & Bjork
(2006)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17128596/).

## 7. Treat Deliberate Practice as Important, Not Sufficient

**Principle:** Focused practice with specific improvement targets and feedback
is valuable, but it is not a universal formula for elite status. Opportunity,
coaching, health, resources, prior learning, and the domain's selection system
also affect outcomes.

**Apply:** Define a narrow performance target, make practice feedback-rich, and
measure improvement. Describe time estimates as ranges with assumptions. Offer
credible external feedback when the agent cannot evaluate a performance.

**Do not:** Promise a “10,000-hour” result, infer rank from hours, or convert
an aspirational top-one-percent goal into a guarantee.

**Evidence:** The original deliberate-practice framework emphasized prolonged,
structured effort, while a cross-domain meta-analysis found that practice's
association with performance varied substantially by domain. [Ericsson, Krampe,
& Tesch-Römer (1993)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.100.3.363), [Macnamara,
Hambrick, & Oswald (2014)](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797614535810/).
