# Naming Strategy Reference

Use this reference to turn a broad idea into a naming brief, choose productive creative territories, and judge candidates consistently across different kinds of businesses.

## Start with the naming job

A name can orient, reassure, intrigue, signal taste, or create a distinctive vessel for meaning. It cannot carry the entire positioning by itself. Decide which job matters most:

- **Orient:** make an unfamiliar offer easier to understand.
- **Differentiate:** create distance from a crowded category vocabulary.
- **Signal:** convey authority, warmth, speed, craft, rebellion, or another useful trait.
- **Remember:** produce a compact verbal shape people can retrieve later.
- **Stretch:** remain credible as products, locations, audiences, or markets expand.

State the primary job and at most one secondary job. Trying to maximize every quality usually produces bland names.

## Route by business type

| Business type | Prioritize | Watch for | Domain posture |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| B2B or enterprise technology | credibility, fluency in speech, category distance, product-family stretch | gimmicky futurism, hard spelling, names that sound like features | exact `.com` is strongly preferred unless the market has a credible alternative norm |
| AI product, developer tool, protocol, or open-source project | technical credibility, typeability, spoken handoff, namespace fit, company-to-product architecture | forced technical puns, temporary-model language, package collisions, implied official affiliation | check the domain alongside relevant repositories, package registries, commands, and protocol namespaces |
| Consumer app or ecommerce brand | recall, emotion, mouthfeel, spelling, visual potential | fleeting slang, cute sameness, social confusion | exact short domain; test the TLD and handle together when social discovery matters |
| Local service, shop, or hospitality | immediate trust, locality, phone test, useful description | over-abstraction, geographic lock-in, search confusion | `.com` plus the relevant country-code option; a clear modifier may be acceptable if intentional |
| Professional or regulated service | trust, dignity, accuracy, legal or sector rules | unprovable claims, frivolity, misleading specialization | conservative exact domain; check professional naming rules and entity registries |
| Marketplace, network, or platform | neutrality across participants, movement, scale, ecosystem stretch | favoring one side, narrow transaction language | exact global domain with room for multiple product lines |
| Nonprofit, community, or public-interest organization | mission resonance, dignity, inclusion, easy advocacy | savior language, vagueness, cultural appropriation | exact `.org` is usually the first choice; check charity and entity registries |
| Creative studio, agency, or practice | point of view, taste, memorability, portfolio breadth | fashionable sameness, self-important abstraction | exact domain or a deliberate studio descriptor; explain any modifier |
| Parent or holding company | permanence, neutrality, acquisition stretch | names tied to one product or founder moment | exact `.com` and legal-entity search matter more than category description |
| Physical product, food, or beauty brand | sensory character, shelf recall, pronunciation, packaging potential | ingredient or benefit claims, crowded trademark classes | exact consumer-facing domain; check retail, marketplace, and trademark collisions |

These are starting points, not formulas. Route hybrids by the audience that must remember and say the name most often.

## Define name architecture first

Clarify whether the new name belongs to:

- a legal company;
- a customer-facing master brand;
- one product or service;
- a location or franchise concept;
- an endorsed sub-brand;
- a parent brand with future children;
- a protocol or open-source project;
- a CLI command, SDK, package, repository, endpoint, or module family.

Do not force one name to do incompatible jobs. A plain legal entity can own a more expressive product brand, and a descriptive service line can sit beneath a distinctive master brand.

For a technical naming system, sketch the hierarchy before choosing the root:

```text
Company or foundation
└── Platform or protocol
    ├── CLI command
    ├── SDK and package names
    ├── Product modules
    └── Repository organization
```

The root does not need to repeat unchanged everywhere, but the family should feel intentional and remain typeable in the conventions of each surface.

## Build distinct creative territories

Use at least four territories rooted in the strategy. Useful territory shapes include:

- the customer's transformation or destination;
- the mechanism, ritual, or motion behind the value;
- a metaphor from nature, craft, navigation, architecture, music, or another credible world;
- the relationship or community the business creates;
- the founder's origin, place, material, or authentic story;
- a distinctive coined form built from relevant linguistic roots;
- a surprising real word whose existing meaning becomes an advantage;
- a technical primitive, system behavior, or protocol relationship when the audience will understand it.

Name each territory with a short thesis. If two territories produce interchangeable names, they are one territory.

## Vary the construction

Generate across several constructions:

- real words used directly or metaphorically;
- compounds and compressed phrases;
- suggestive names that imply the benefit without describing it;
- coined words with intentional roots and sound symbolism;
- founder, place, or provenance names when they add durable meaning;
- descriptive names when clarity is more valuable than broad distinctiveness;
- technical wordplay or abbreviations that remain pronounceable and meaningful outside the implementation detail.

Do not treat random letter substitution, fashionable suffixes, or borrowed classical roots as strategy. A coined name should have an explainable verbal logic, not merely an open domain. Never generate the explanation after the fact; if the root, sound, or metaphor did not lead to the name, do not claim that it did.

## Generate with discipline

For each territory:

1. Expand the semantic field: objects, actions, textures, emotions, places, myths, tools, and outcomes.
2. Move laterally into credible source worlds instead of staying inside category jargon.
3. Combine roots, sounds, and images in several construction types.
4. Say each promising candidate in a sentence before keeping it.
5. Keep duplicates and near-duplicates out of the raw count.

Candidate count follows the mode. Fast naming needs at least 15 genuinely distinct internal candidates. Expand toward 40–60 for a comprehensive engagement, a weak first pool, or a validation refill. Count conceptual diversity, not spelling variations. The user should see only the strongest names appropriate to the mode.

## Test technical and product-family names

When the name may appear in software or a product system, test the relevant forms:

- lowercase CLI command and common flags;
- npm, PyPI, crates.io, container, or other package conventions;
- repository organization and slug;
- import statement, SDK class, environment variable, and API endpoint;
- company, platform, product, and module combinations;
- spoken handoff, such as “install ___” or “open the ___ repository”;
- pluralization, casing, abbreviation, and likely autocomplete behavior.

A clever technical name fails if users cannot hear it once and install the correct package. A clean package name does not rescue a weak company brand. Treat surface usability as a contextual gate, not a universal scoring dimension.

For source-inspired work, write down the source mechanism before generating. Preserve the transferable mechanism—such as a networking pun, compact command shape, or domain relationship—without copying proprietary identity or implying endorsement.

## Score the strategic quality

Score each dimension from 0 to 5, then apply the weight. A score below 3 in distinctiveness, memorability, or spelling and pronunciation is normally disqualifying.

| Dimension | Weight | Question |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Strategic fit | 20 | Does the name reinforce the position and naming job? |
| Distinctiveness | 20 | Is it meaningfully different from category conventions and competitors? |
| Memorability | 15 | Can the intended audience retrieve it after one or two exposures? |
| Sound and sayability | 10 | Is it pleasant, confident, and unambiguous when spoken? |
| Spelling and transmission | 10 | Can someone hear it once and type it correctly? |
| Stretch | 10 | Can it hold future offers, markets, or audiences without becoming absurd? |
| Emotional character | 10 | Does it create the intended feeling without an unwanted one? |
| Searchability | 5 | Can the brand plausibly own a clean search result rather than disappear into noise? |

Convert the weighted result to 100. Treat domain, legal, cultural, and material category collisions as gates outside the score.

## Stress-test finalists

Run every finalist through:

- **Radio test:** say it once without spelling it; can someone type it?
- **Memory test:** revisit it after a break; is it retrievable without the list?
- **Sentence test:** “I work at ___,” “I bought it from ___,” and “Try ___.”
- **Search test:** does an exact query have a realistic path to brand ownership?
- **Shelf test:** does it stand apart when listed beside five competitors?
- **Stretch test:** attach two plausible future offers or locations.
- **Failure test:** list the likeliest mispronunciation, misspelling, joke, and negative reading.
- **Translation test:** inspect launch languages, transliterations, and sound-alikes.
- **System test:** when relevant, attach the company, product, CLI, SDK, and two plausible modules.
- **Affiliation test:** ask whether the name could falsely suggest sponsorship, compatibility, or official status.

Do not explain away a repeated failure. A name that needs a paragraph before it sounds good has not earned the recommendation.
